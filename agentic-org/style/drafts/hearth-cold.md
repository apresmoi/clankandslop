The Work of Staying

War, heat and failing utilities press on the same human bargain: a place should be able to sustain the people who live there.

Metinvest said a Russian ballistic strike killed workers at Kametstal, its steel plant in Kamianske, and forced all production to stop. The damage extended through sinter and blast-furnace production, energy equipment and transport. No restart time was given. [E1] For the surviving workers, the uncertainty now includes when there will be another shift.

In Cuba, the U.S. Embassy reported a significant increase in diarrheal illness and associated it with continuing degradation of water and energy infrastructure. Its September 4 health alert identified no pathogen. [E3] The warning connected sickness to the systems people depend on before they ever need a doctor. Keeping people well becomes harder when water and electricity both need repair.

Greater Sydney faced a different kind of interruption. The New South Wales Rural Fire Service listed Extreme fire danger for September 5 and imposed a Total Fire Ban. Reuters reported unusual spring heat, dry air and gusty northwesterlies; no large destructive Sydney fire had been reported. [E2] The order came before such a loss. Some of the work of keeping a place habitable consists of stopping people from doing what would be harmless on another day.

Perhaps these are only separate emergencies that rhyme. A fire ban can show a public service doing its job; a missile can stop a plant whose workers did everything right. Neither establishes the prolonged deterioration described in Cuba. There may be no common retreat in the capacity to sustain daily life. A difficult day need not mean that a place is becoming harder to inhabit.

In Nepal, police said 4,898 people remained missing on the eleventh day after an ice-rock avalanche near the border with Tibet. Rescue teams continued working at hydropower and tunnel sites. Those conducting temporary burials had taken samples for identification. [E4] That last task carries a duty beyond restoring a service or reopening a workplace: a family may still learn where its dead have been laid.

The people taking those samples are doing something that belongs beside repair and rescue, though it cannot undo the loss. They are preserving the possibility of an answer. Around a hearth, an empty place can belong to someone who is late, someone who has gone away, or someone who will never return. A family waiting for a name cannot yet know how to sit beside it.
